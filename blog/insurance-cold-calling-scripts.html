Insurance cold calling scripts & objection rebuttals

Cold Call X · Published August 29, 2026 · 9 min read

Insurance is one of the few products people hope to never use — which is exactly why cold calling still works in it. Nobody shops for life insurance on a Tuesday afternoon, but a renewal notice, a new mortgage, or a hire raises the question today. The agents who win the phone are the ones with a specific reason to call, a short ask, and a system that keeps them dialing past the first twenty refusals. Below: openers by line of coverage, gatekeeper lines, word-for-word rebuttals, voicemails, and the daily workflow that ties it together.

The anatomy of an insurance cold call

Every script below follows the same skeleton. Keep each beat short — the whole opener should take 20–25 seconds before your first question:

  • Name + agency + city (10 seconds). Trust starts with who and where, not with a pitch.
  • Reason for the call — something about them: renewal timing, a life event, a new business, a rate environment.
  • Permission bridge: “Did I catch you at a bad time?” or “Give me 30 seconds and you can tell me no.”
  • One qualifying question. Get them talking; quotes come after questions, not before.
Compliance first: insurance cold calling sits under TCPA rules, the National Do Not Call Registry, state mini-DNC lists, and — for Medicare products — CMS marketing rules. Openers by line of coverage

Home & auto (P&C renewal or rate angle)

“Hi, is this [First name]? This is [Your name] with [Agency] here in [City]. The reason for my call — carriers in [State] have been repricing home and auto policies this year, and I'm doing a free rate check for folks in your zip before their [Month] renewal. Quick question so I don't waste your time: are you currently with a big national carrier, or an independent?”

Either answer works. National carrier → “That's exactly who I've been beating on price lately.” Independent → “Good — then you know the value of an agent; I'd just like ten minutes at your renewal to make sure you're still in the right spot.”

Life insurance (milestone angle)

“Hi [First name], this is [Your name] with [Agency] in [City]. Totally cold call — I work with families in [Area] on making sure the mortgage and the kids are covered if something happens to a breadwinner. Most people I call already have some coverage, so my question is simple: if you passed away tomorrow, would your current policy carry the house for more than a few years?”

The “most people I call already have some” line defuses the “I have coverage” brush-off before it happens. Never discuss rates, benefits, or specific policy terms on a first cold call — book the appointment.

Commercial lines (new hire / new location angle)

“Hi, [First name]? [Your name] with [Agency] — we're a commercial broker here in [City]. I saw [Business name] is expanding/you've been hiring this year. As headcount and locations change, most generic policies quietly fall out of step with the business. Who handles your insurance decisions — you, or an office manager?”

Then: “Fair enough. What I'd like to do is a 15-minute review of your current coverage gaps — no pitch, just a checklist — around your renewal. When does that renew?”

Gatekeeper lines

For commercial and high-net-worth prospects you'll often hit a gatekeeper. Treat them as an ally, never an obstacle:

  • “Hi, I'm sorry, I don't have your name — I'm [Your name] with [Agency]. Who handles the insurance decisions there? … Great — is [Name] in, or is there a better time to catch them?”
  • If pressed on purpose: “It's about their upcoming renewal — honestly, they'll want to hear the timing. Can you put me through, or should I try after two?”
  • Always get the decision-maker's name and the best time, then actually call back at that time. Log it — this is exactly what a follow-up reminder is for.

Objection rebuttals, word for word

“I already have an agent.”

“That's honestly who I'm hoping you're with — a local agent means you already value advice over a website. I'm not asking you to switch anything today. Renewals are the one time a second set of eyes takes ten minutes and either confirms you're fine or finds a gap. When's yours?”

“Not interested.”

“No problem at all — most people aren't until something changes. Can I ask one quick thing: when does your current policy renew? … Perfect, I'll call you a few weeks before it — if rates haven't moved, I'll leave you alone and you'll have confirmed a fair price either way. Fair?”

“It's too expensive / you can't beat my rate.”

“Maybe I can't — rate isn't the only thing that moves, though. Coverage gaps and re-shopped renewals are where people usually save, not the base premium. Send me your current declaration page and I'll tell you straight in ten minutes whether there's anything there. If not, you've lost nothing.”

“I need to talk to my spouse.”

“Please do — this is a both-of-you decision. What I'd hate is for you to be the only one with the information. Can we get the two of you on a fifteen-minute call this week, evenings included? Is Tuesday or Thursday better?”

“Just email me something.”

“Happy to — but a PDF can't ask you questions, and that's where the value is. Tell you what: I'll email you a one-page checklist right after this call so you have something in hand, and I'll follow up Thursday to hear which of the three items surprised you. Does that work?” (Then actually send it and set the follow-up before you hang up.)

“Let me think about it.”

“Of course. Usually 'let me think about it' means one of two things — the timing isn't right, or the value isn't clear yet. Which one is it for you?” Then handle whichever they name. If timing: book the callback. If value: offer the ten-minute review, not more talking.

Voicemail scripts (15–20 seconds)

Leave a voicemail roughly every third dial — never on every attempt:

  • Consumer: “Hi [First name], [Your name] with [Agency] here in [City]. I'm doing renewal rate checks for [Zip] homes ahead of your [Month] renewal — nothing you need to prepare. My number is [number], or I'll try you again [day] around [time]. Again, [Your name], [number].”
  • Commercial: “[First name], [Your name] from [Agency] — calling about [Business]'s coverage as you grow this year. I'll send a short email with the two gaps I see most in your industry; if they don't apply, ignore me. [Number].”

The daily workflow that makes scripts work

Scripts fail from inconsistency, not wording. A solo agent who books appointments usually runs something like this:

  1. Build the list once — aged leads, expiring renewals, a purchased list of local homeowners or businesses. Import the CSV (or a Google Sheet) in one shot; no retyping.
  2. Scrub it — keep a Do Not Call suppression list inside your dialer so anyone who said “never” never gets dialed again.
  3. Dial in order, not in mood — 50–100 dials in one focused block beats sporadic calls all day. A power dialer that walks the list one number at a time from your own iPhone keeps you a live person on every call — the low-risk side of the compliance line (more in our iPhone auto dialer guide).
  4. Tag as you go — Hot / Warm / Cold. A “call back before renewal” lead should never end up back at the top of the raw list.
  5. Set the follow-up immediately — while still on the line, calendar the callback. Tomorrow-you cannot remember Thursday's “call me in two weeks.”
  6. Rebut from templates — text and email follow-up templates with name and company fields turn “just email me something” into a booked review in two taps.

Compliance quick-reference (not legal advice)

  • Calling windows: federal rules generally allow 8 a.m.–9 p.m. in the recipient's local time; some states are stricter.
  • National DNC Registry: sales calls to registered numbers are violations with per-call penalties; build your own suppression list in addition, and honor opt-outs forever.
  • Cell phones: prerecorded/artificial-voice calls and autodialed calls to cells require prior express written consent — the high-risk zone. Live person, your own number, manually sequenced lists stay on the safe side.
  • Medicare: CMS marketing rules add consent, call-recording, and scope-of-appointment requirements — get trained before cold calling Medicare products.

Bottom line

Insurance cold calling rewards three things: a timely reason to call, a rebuttal you've practiced out loud until it sounds unrehearsed, and enough consistent dials that the law of averages starts working for you. Insurance cold calling, answered

What is the best time to cold call insurance prospects?+

Late morning (10–12) and mid-to-late afternoon (4–6) in the prospect's local time zone; for commercial lines, business hours minus Mondays and Friday afternoons. Stay inside the 8 a.m.–9 p.m. local calling window.

Is cold calling legal for insurance agents?+

Yes, when done manually and compliantly: TCPA, National DNC Registry, state mini-DNC lists, and CMS rules for Medicare all apply. Keep a suppression list, honor opt-outs, and never prerecord or autodial cells without consent. Not legal advice.

What is a good cold call opener for insurance?+

A specific, timely reason: renewal date, life event, new hire or location. Name yourself, agency, and city in ten seconds, ask permission to continue, then ask one qualifying question.

How many dials does an insurance agent need per day?+

Many solo agents plan 50–100 dials a day to book a handful of appointments, given typical 10–20% connect rates. Consistency beats volume spikes — which is why sequenced dialing beats scattered notes.

Can I use an auto dialer for insurance cold calling?+

A manual-assist dialer that only calls numbers you imported, one at a time, from your own number keeps you on the low-risk side — you're a live person on every call. Predictive multi-line dialers and prerecorded voice are where TCPA risk concentrates.
