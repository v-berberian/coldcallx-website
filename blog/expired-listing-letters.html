Expired listing letter templates that win the re-listing

Cold Call X · Published August 2026 · 9 min read

When a listing expires, the homeowner gets a stack of mail from agents within a week — and throws most of it away. The expired listing letter templates below are built to survive that stack: they acknowledge the failure without insulting anyone, name a specific reason the home didn't sell, and offer a plan that's visibly different from what was already tried. You'll get six field-tested formats — just-expired, withdrawn-from-the-market, the second letter, short-mail, email, and text versions — plus the timing sequence that ties letters to live calls. That sequence matters more than the words: the letter earns the credibility, the call earns the appointment, and the follow-up earns the signature.

What makes an expired listing letter work

Every market is different, but the letters that get callbacks share five traits:

  • Speed. Mailed the same day the listing expires, so it lands while the frustration is fresh and the decision is open.
  • A specific diagnosis. Price, presentation, or marketing — pick one and explain it in plain language. A letter that says "I have buyers!" says nothing.
  • No agent-bashing. The seller picked that agent. Insulting them insults the seller's judgment and gets the letter binned.
  • One proof point. A nearby sale you handled, a stat, a concrete example — earned trust beats claimed trust.
  • One low-friction ask. "Fifteen minutes, no obligation" — never "list with me."

For the phone side of the same play, use the openers and objection handlers in the expired listing scripts guide — the letter templates below are designed to pair with those calls, not replace them.

The six letter templates

1. The just-expired letter (day 0)

The workhorse. Empathy, one diagnosis, one proof, one ask. Keep it under 250 words — long letters read like campaigns; short letters read like people.

Dear [Owner Name],

I noticed your home at [Address] recently came off the market, and I'd guess the last few months didn't go the way you'd hoped. I'm sorry — that's a frustrating place to be.

Homes on your street fail to sell for one of three reasons: price, presentation, or marketing. When I look at your listing, what stands out is [one specific observation — e.g., "only two of the eleven photos showed the backyard, and none were taken after staging"]. That's fixable — often without spending a dollar.

Last spring I re-listed a home two blocks from yours that had sat for five months. We changed the story the listing told, and it was under contract in eleven days at [or near] the original asking price. I'd be glad to show you exactly what we changed.

If you're still hoping to sell, I'd welcome fifteen minutes — no obligation, no pressure to decide anything. I'll bring a simple plan for [Address] and you can judge it on its merits.

Warmly,
[Your Name]
[Brokerage] · [Phone]

2. The withdrawn-listing letter

The seller pulled the home before expiration — usually frustration plus a life event (new job delayed, divorce paused, kid's school year). They need permission to rest, and a plan for later.

Dear [Owner Name],

I saw that [Address] was recently withdrawn from the market. Sometimes that's the right call — selling on a forced timeline rarely gets the best result.

Whenever you're ready to try again, the good news is that the first attempt taught you exactly what to fix. From the outside, the two things I'd change are [one specific pricing/presentation point] and [one specific marketing point]. Neither requires a big spend — mostly a different plan.

There's no rush on my end. If it would help to have a second opinion on timing — what your home is worth today versus in the spring — I'm happy to put that together with no strings attached.

All the best,
[Your Name]
[Brokerage] · [Phone]

3. The second letter (day 7–10)

Most agents quit after one. The second letter is where you win, because it arrives after the seller's stack has thinned and often after they've given up on selling. Reference your first letter — it proves you're organized, which is exactly the trait they didn't get last time.

Dear [Owner Name],

I wrote to you last week about [Address] — I'm the agent with the fifteen-minute, no-obligation offer. I'm writing once more because I've been watching your neighborhood: [one fresh market fact — e.g., "three homes within half a mile went under contract in the last two weeks, all in under 21 days"]. The buyers are there. The plan is what changes.

If you'd like to see what a different plan looks like for your home — including the exact changes I'd make and what I'd list at — reply to this letter, call, or text me any time at [Phone]. If I don't hear from you, I'll take that as your answer and won't write again.

Either way, I wish you the best with the house.

[Your Name]
[Brokerage] · [Phone]

That last promise — "I won't write again" — is deliberate. It reframes the second letter as professionalism instead of pursuit, and it's the single highest-leverage sentence in the sequence.

4. The short-mail note

A handwritten-style card, three sentences, mailed between phone attempts. Response rates beat long letters because it doesn't look like marketing at all.

[Owner Name] — I drove past [Address] yesterday and it struck me again what a great layout that home has for [buyers with kids / commuters / downsizers]. If the sale left a bad taste, I understand. But when you're ready, I have an idea for selling it that I think you'd like. — [Your Name], [Brokerage], [Phone]

5. The email version

For owners you have an email address for (from prior correspondence, your sphere, or their agent's now-expired listing flyer). Subject lines matter more than the body — write like a neighbor, not a newsletter.

Subject: quick note about your house on [Street Name]

Hi [Owner Name],

I'm a local agent — you may have seen my letter about [Address]. One thought I didn't have room for: homes in [Neighborhood] that sold this quarter went for [X], and the ones that sat all shared one trait: they launched with photos taken before [staging/seasonal cleanup]. Yours has better bones than most of them.

If you're up for it, I'll send over a one-page "what I'd do differently" plan — free, no call required. Just reply "plan" and it's yours.

[Your Name] · [Phone]

The "just reply 'plan'" mechanic converts far better than a calendar link — it asks for a word, not a meeting. For consent-safe SMS follow-up wording, see the real estate text scripts guide.

6. The text version

Only where you have consent or a prior relationship — otherwise cold texts to expireds cross both the FTC's CAN-SPAM line and TCPA consent rules for texts. Where it's clean, keep it to two sentences:

Hi [Owner Name] — [Your Name] from [Brokerage], I also mailed you a letter about [Address]. If you'd ever want my one-page "what I'd do differently" plan, just reply PLAN and I'll send it over. No calls unless you ask.

The sequence that ties it together

Letters alone get a fraction of the replies that a letter-plus-call system gets. Run it like a campaign:

  • Day 0: Listing expires. Mail letter #1 same day. First call attempt that afternoon — the live openers are in the expired listing call scripts.
  • Day 1–2: Second call attempt at a different time of day; leave a short voicemail under 25 seconds — the format is in the voicemail scripts guide. Your letter lands today or tomorrow; the voicemail now refers to a physical letter the seller can go find.
  • Day 3–5: Short-mail note arrives mid-week. Third call attempt.
  • Day 7–10: Letter #2 with the fresh market fact and the "I won't write again" close.
  • Day 14: Final call attempt. Tag the lead Warm or Cold and set a 60-day callback — a large share of expired sellers re-list in month two or three, and the agent who's still standing then gets the listing.

Quick takeaways

  • Mail letter #1 the same day the listing expires — speed beats polish.
  • One diagnosis, one proof point, one low-friction ask. Under 250 words.
  • Never insult the previous agent; you're insulting the seller's judgment.
  • The second letter with "I won't write again" is the highest-conversion single piece.
  • Letters + calls in one sequence: day 0 letter/call, day 3–5 short-mail, day 7–10 letter #2, day 14 final call, day 60 re-check.

Expired listing letters, answered

How soon should I mail a letter after a listing expires?+

Same day. The letter lands within 2–4 days, right when the seller is most open to a new plan. Same-day letters beat batch weekly mailings because the seller's attention window is the first week.

Do expired listing letters still work in 2026?+

Yes — as part of a sequence. Same-day letter + live call + second letter 7–10 days later beats any single touch. Letters outlast calls because they sit on the counter as physical proof of effort.

What should an expired listing letter say?+

Acknowledge the failure without blaming anyone, name one specific reason the home didn't sell, offer a visibly different plan with one proof point, and ask for fifteen minutes with no obligation. Under 250 words, hand-signed.

Is contacting expired listings allowed?+

Generally yes — expiration is public record. Honor do-not-call requests and the National DNC Registry for calls/texts, and pull owner details from public records rather than MLS data, since some markets' MLS rules restrict prospecting from MLS feeds. Not legal advice.

Should I call or send a letter first?+

Both, the same day. Call first and mention "a letter is already in the mail" — the letter then arrives as confirmation instead of a cold approach, and answer rates go up on the follow-up calls.
