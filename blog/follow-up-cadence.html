Follow-up cadence: how many times to call a lead, and how far apart

By Vadym Berberian · Cold Call X · October 2026 · 7 min read

Most leads don't pick up the first time. Whether they ever do depends less on your script than on what happens after that first unanswered dial: when you call again, at what hour, whether you leave a message, and when you stop. That plan is your follow-up cadence. This guide gives you a three-week cadence you can copy, what to change after each call outcome, and the state rules that cap how often you can call.

Short answer: call a cold lead six to eight times over about three weeks, each attempt on a different day and at a different time of day, with a voicemail on the first attempt, around day 7 and on the last one. Then drop to a monthly check-in for two or three months and stop. Never stack more than three calls in 24 hours: Florida, Oklahoma and Maryland make that a legal limit. On an iPhone, Cold Call X schedules the next attempt from every call outcome, reminds you each morning and only dials leads during their own daytime.

How many times should you call a lead?

There is no magic number, but there is a shape that works. One attempt is not a cadence: a lead who missed your call was driving, in a meeting or screening an unknown number, not saying no. Calling at different hours on different days is what turns "no answer" into a conversation, and the best time to cold call guide covers which windows to rotate through.

The other side matters as much: a cadence with no end turns into the same twenty stale leads eating the top of your list every morning. Decide up front how many attempts a lead gets before it slows down, and how many before it leaves the list.

Three things make this work. The gaps grow, so early attempts catch people while your first message is fresh and later ones don't feel like pressure. The hour rotates, so you aren't calling the same person at the same busy moment every time. And voicemails are rationed: three over two weeks is plenty, and scripts for each one are in voicemail scripts that get callbacks. If a lead has agreed to texts, a short text after a voicemail helps; without that consent, don't text.

Working a fresh inbound lead instead of a cold list? Compress the start: call within minutes, again later the same day, then fall into the schedule above.

Change the cadence after every call outcome

The table is for leads you haven't reached. The moment a call ends with a result, the result decides what happens next:

  • No Answer: stay on the schedule; move to the next attempt at a different hour.
  • Voicemail: give it a day or two before the next call, so they have time to call back.
  • Callback: leave the schedule. Call at exactly the time they gave you; it is the most valuable follow-up on your list.
  • Interested: book the next step while you're on the call, then follow the meeting or appointment, not the cadence.
  • Not Interested: stop the sequence. If it was "not now", one check-in months later is reasonable; if it was "never", treat it as do not call.
  • Wrong Number: that number is done. Try the lead's other numbers if you have them.
  • Do Not Call: remove the number from every list at once, and for good.

The full list of outcomes, and what each should trigger, is in call disposition codes.

The rules that cap your cadence

In the US, telephone solicitations to residential numbers may only be made between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. in the called person's local time (47 CFR 64.1200(c)(1)). Three states also limit how often you can call the same person:

  • Florida: no more than three commercial telephone solicitation calls from any number to a person in a 24-hour period on the same subject, regardless of the number used, and no calls before 8 a.m. or after 8 p.m. (Fla. Stat. § 501.616(6)).
  • Oklahoma: the same three-in-24-hours limit and 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. window under the Telephone Solicitation Act of 2022 (HB 3168, enrolled).
  • Maryland: no more than three calls to the same person in 24 hours on the same subject, and none between 8 p.m. and 8 a.m., under the Stop the Spam Calls Act of 2023 (Chapter 413 of 2023).

Two practical consequences. Double-dialing counts: calling twice in a row, then once more after lunch, is already three calls. And switching numbers doesn't reset the count in Florida, because the limit applies "regardless of the phone number used". Spreading attempts across days, as in the table above, keeps you well inside every limit.

When someone asks not to be called, US rules require sellers to honor the request within a reasonable time of no more than 10 business days and keep honoring it for 5 years (47 CFR 64.1200(d)(3) and (d)(6)). State-by-state calling windows are in legal calling hours; for other countries see the Canada, UK and Australia guides. Which calls these rules cover, and what exemptions apply, depends on who you call and why. This is general information, not legal advice.

Follow-up cadence checklist

  • Six to eight attempts over about three weeks for leads you haven't reached.
  • A different day and a different hour for each attempt.
  • Voicemail on attempts 1, 4 and 6, not every time.
  • Never more than three calls to one person in 24 hours, double-dials included.

  • Monthly check-ins after the sequence, then remove the lead.
  • Do-not-call requests off every list at once.

Follow-up cadence questions, answered

What is a follow-up cadence in sales?+

A follow-up cadence is the planned sequence of attempts you make to reach a lead: how many calls, how many days apart, at what times of day, and when you leave a voicemail or send a text. A good cadence spreads attempts across different days and hours, changes after every call outcome, and has a clear point where you stop or slow down.

How many times should you call a lead before giving up?+

For a cold lead who hasn't answered, six to eight attempts over about three weeks is a workable default, each on a different day and at a different time of day. After that, move the lead to a monthly check-in for two or three months, then stop. A lead who says no or asks not to be called again leaves the cadence at once.

How long should you wait between follow-up calls?+

Spread the first attempts one to three days apart (for example day 1, 2, 4, 7, 10 and 14), then widen the gap to weekly and monthly. Calling again on a different day at a different hour reaches more people than redialing the same afternoon, and it keeps you clear of state limits on calls in a 24-hour period.

Is it illegal to call someone more than three times a day?+

In some US states, for telephone solicitations, yes. Florida bans more than three commercial telephone solicitation calls from any number to a person in 24 hours on the same subject, and Oklahoma and Maryland have similar three-in-24-hours limits; all three also limit calling to 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. Federal rules allow 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. in the called person's local time. Double-dialing counts as two calls. This is general information, not legal advice.

Should you leave a voicemail on every follow-up call?+

No. Leave a short voicemail on the first attempt, skip it on the next two or three, leave a second one about a week in, and a brief final one when you close out the sequence. A voicemail on every call reads as pressure and adds little; the calls themselves do the work.
