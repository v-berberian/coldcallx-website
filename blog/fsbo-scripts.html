FSBO scripts that actually work

Cold Call X · Updated August 2026 · 9 min read

Every FSBO you dial has already made one decision about you: they don't want an agent. That's the whole list, and it's why most FSBO scripts fail in the first five seconds. The scripts below are built for that reality — useful first, pitch later, and structured so the seller keeps talking instead of hanging up.

Why FSBOs are different from every other list

An expired listing seller hired an agent and got disappointed. A skip-traced absentee owner never thought about selling at all. A FSBO is the only list where the owner made a deliberate, public decision to avoid you — and then advertised that decision on a yard sign.

That changes the math of the call. They aren't screening for whether you're competent — they're screening for one thing: is this another agent trying to get my listing? The moment your first sentence pattern-matches to an agent pitch, they stop listening and start hunting for the exit line. Most have taken four or five of these calls this week, so the filter is fast and unforgiving.

The upside is that FSBOs are one of the highest-intent lists in real estate. They have a deadline, a price in their head, and a house already prepped to sell. You don't have to manufacture motivation — you only have to survive the filter. So the posture is useful-first. Not clever, not high-pressure, not "just checking in." Useful. Bring a buyer, bring information, bring a second opinion on price — bring something the owner would have wanted even if you never asked for the listing.

The opening 10 seconds

Everything is decided here. Say your name, say why you're calling, and hand them control of the conversation before they have to take it.

"Hi, is this {First Name}? {First Name}, this is {Your Name} — I'll be straight with you, this is a cold call about the house on {Street}. I know you're selling it yourself. I'm not calling to talk you out of that. I had one question about the property — do you have thirty seconds?"

Four beats, each doing a specific job:

  • "I'll be straight with you, this is a cold call." — They already know. Naming it first removes what they were about to accuse you of, and almost nobody else on their call log does it.
  • "I know you're selling it yourself." — Proves you aren't blind-dialing, and signals you're about to address the elephant instead of pretending it isn't there.
  • "I'm not calling to talk you out of that." — The line that saves the call. It disarms the exact objection they've loaded and are waiting to fire.
  • "Do you have thirty seconds?" — A small, specific ask. Saying yes costs nothing, and once they have, you've got permission instead of tolerance. Thirty beats a minute — a minute sounds like a pitch.

Deliver it at a normal, slightly unhurried pace. Fast talking is the loudest tell that you're reading, and FSBOs hear it every day.

Six FSBO scripts that work in different situations

One script does not cover a FSBO list. A sign that went up yesterday and one that's been sitting six weeks are completely different conversations. Match the script to the situation.

1. The buyer-first opener

"I work with buyers in {area} and I saw your place on {Street}. I'm not calling about listing it — I'm calling because I've got people looking in that price range. Quick question: if I brought you a buyer who was a real fit, would you work with an agent on that, or are you strictly selling solo?"

Why this works: you're offering, not asking. A FSBO will almost always take a buyer conversation, because a buyer is the entire reason the sign went up. The closing question doubles as a qualifier — "I'd work with an agent if they brought a buyer" is the most common answer, and it's an open door. Only say this if you genuinely work with buyers in that range; inventing one burns the name permanently.

2. The straight-shooter

"{First Name}, I'm going to be honest with you because you're going to figure it out anyway — I'm an agent. You're selling on your own and I respect that; a lot of people pull it off. I'd rather be useful than pushy, so here's my offer: I'll send you the last three comparable sales on your street, no strings. If you sell it yourself, great, you got free data. Fair enough?"

Why this works: it volunteers the thing they're suspicious of. Sellers are braced for a disguise, and dropping it resets the interaction. The no-strings offer is small enough to accept without feeling committed, and it buys you a reason to call back next week with something in hand. Best opener on the list for anyone who hates sounding salesy.

3. The curiosity opener for a fresh FSBO

"Saw the sign go up on {Street} — how's it going so far? Getting decent traffic, or is it mostly agents calling you?"

Why this works: in the first week or two the owner is optimistic and wants to talk about it. The question is genuinely open, and the second half — "or is it mostly agents calling you?" — is a shared joke that puts you on their side of the table. Usually you get a laugh and an honest answer. Then stop talking and let them run. On a fresh FSBO your only job is to be memorable and pleasant; they are not listing this week no matter what you say. You're planting a flag for week four.

4. The aged FSBO, 30 to 60 days in

"{First Name}, it's {Your Name} — I know the place on {Street} has been up for about {X} weeks now. I'm not calling to give you a hard time about it. I talk to a lot of owners at this exact point and it's usually one of two things: either the showings aren't coming, or they're coming and nobody's writing. Which one is it for you?"

Why this works: by week five the yard sign has stopped being exciting and started being a chore — lowballs, no-shows, and a stack of agent calls. Naming the frustration accurately earns instant credibility, and a two-option question is far easier to answer than "how's it going." Whichever one they pick hands you the diagnosis. Never say I told you so, even implicitly; pride is the last thing holding the FSBO decision together, and poking it ends the call.

5. The price-and-showings diagnostic

"Two quick things and I'll get out of your hair. How many showings have you had in the last two weeks? … And what are you asking? … Okay. Here's what that combination usually tells me — {when showings are low, it's almost always price or exposure; when showings are high and no offers, it's almost always condition or terms}. Want me to look at your comps and tell you which one it is?"

Why this works: it makes you sound like a professional rather than a caller, and both numbers are facts the seller already knows and is happy to say out loud. The diagnosis is real — traffic versus offers genuinely separates a pricing problem from a product problem. Ending on a question about their specific house makes the appointment feel like their idea. Save it for the second or third call; asking for numbers before you've earned trust reads as an interrogation.

6. The second-touch follow-up call

"{First Name}, {Your Name} again — we talked about {Street} a couple weeks back. No pitch, I just said I'd check in and I keep my word. Where are you at with it? … Still going solo? Good for you. I'll get out of your way — mind if I check back in a couple weeks in case anything changes?"

Why this works: "I said I'd check in and I keep my word" is the strongest sentence in FSBO prospecting — a small demonstration of the exact quality they'll be shopping for when they finally hire someone. It also asks permission to call again, which converts every future call from a cold call into an expected one. Unglamorous, and it produces more listings than the five above it.

Handling the objections FSBOs actually give you

"I'm not paying a commission."

"I get it, and I'm not asking you to pay one today. Serious question though — if a buyer came in at a number that put more in your pocket after commission than what you'd net today, would you still say no? That's the only version of this worth talking about."

The reasoning: arguing about the percentage is a fight you lose. Net proceeds changes the unit of measurement from a fee to a bottom line — the number they actually care about. Most sellers have never run that arithmetic, and the question is honest rather than rhetorical.

"I'm not interested."

"Fair enough — I called you out of nowhere. Before I go: are you not interested in agents in general, or not interested right now while you're giving it a shot yourself?"

The reasoning: "not interested" is a reflex, not a position. The clarifying question is genuinely curious, and "right now" is a yes with a timer on it. Even if the call ends there, you've learned whether the name belongs in your follow-up rotation or your do-not-call notes.

"I'll call you when I need an agent."

"Deal — and I'd honestly rather you did. One thing though: everybody says that and then when the time comes they don't remember which of the eleven agents to call. How about this — I check in every couple weeks, no pitch, and when you're ready you already know who I am. Sound reasonable?"

The reasoning: it accepts their answer instead of fighting it, then points out the flaw in a way that's obviously true. You're not asking for a commitment, you're asking for permission to stay visible — a much smaller yes, and the one that actually matters.

"How did you get my number?"

"Your listing — you've got it posted publicly with the number on it. I work {area} and I reach out to owners directly. If you'd rather I didn't call again, tell me and you're off my list for good."

The reasoning: answer plainly and immediately. Any hedging turns a question into a complaint. Offering to remove them costs nothing, defuses the annoyance, and the offer is one you must honor — write it down the second they say it.

"I've already got an agent lined up."

"Smart to have a backup plan. Is that someone you've already signed with, or someone you'd call if the solo thing doesn't pan out? … Got it. Well, if that changes I'd love a shot at it — can I keep you on my check-in list either way?"

The reasoning: "lined up" is almost never "signed" — usually it's a cousin, a friend from church, or an agent they spoke to once. Signed versus considered is the whole ballgame, and asking directly is neither rude nor pushy. If they really are signed, thank them and move on.

"I'm going to try it solo for 30 days first."

"Honestly, that's a reasonable plan — the first month is exactly when a well-priced place should move. Can I ask what you're asking? … Okay. Here's what I'll do: I'll check back right at your 30-day mark, and if it's gone, congratulations, nobody will be happier. If it's not, you'll want the comp data and I'll already have it. Fair?"

The reasoning: don't argue with the 30 days — endorse it. You look confident instead of needy, and "right at your 30-day mark" is a concrete, agreed callback that turns a rejection into a scheduled appointment. When you call back, lead with the comp data you promised, not with "so, changed your mind?"

"Not right now — call me in the spring."

"Spring works — a lot of sellers get better prices then anyway. Two quick things so I'm useful when I call back: what price range were you thinking? … And are you doing any prep before then, or keeping it simple? … Got it. I'll check in once before spring with anything that affects your number — sales on your street, rate moves. If something big happens sooner, I'll call. Deal?"

The reasoning: a deferred seller is a follow-up lead, not a dead one. Asking for the price range gives you a number to track comps against — every comp that beats it is a reason to call. "Once before spring" shrinks the commitment to a single touch, which is nearly always accepted. Write the month down and honor it; FSBO callers lose more listings to broken callback promises than to bad scripts.

"We're getting plenty of showings — we're fine."

"That's genuinely good news — traffic is the hard part. Quick question though: how many of those showings have turned into a second visit or an offer conversation? … If it's more showings but no offers, that's usually a condition or terms thing, not a price thing — and that's fixable before you burn the summer traffic window. Want me to tell you the three things buyers say after they leave?"

The reasoning: this seller thinks success equals traffic, and most don't know the showings-to-offers ratio is the real metric. You're not telling them they're failing — you're giving them a diagnostic they've never heard, positioned as fixable. This is the single best objection to hit on the second or third call, once the first wave of showings has passed.

The cadence: where FSBO listings actually come from

Here's the pattern every experienced FSBO caller learns eventually: most for-sale-by-owner sellers who don't sell solo end up listing with an agent, and the agent they pick is usually just the one who was still calling in week seven. So your first call is not a closing attempt — it's the price of admission to a follow-up sequence. A cadence that holds up in practice:

  • Day 1 — first contact. Buyer-first or straight-shooter opener. Goal: a name, a rapport, and permission to call back.
  • Day 4 — deliver whatever you promised. Comps, a market snapshot, a buyer question. Value only, no ask. If you roll to voicemail, use one of the short FSBO voicemail scripts here.
  • Day 10–14 — the "how's it going" check-in. Frustration is starting.
  • Every 7–10 days after — short calls. Thirty seconds is fine. Rotate the reason: a new comparable sale, a rate move, an open house nearby.
  • Week 4–8 — this is the window. Run the price-and-showings diagnostic and ask for the appointment.

The hard part isn't the scripts. It's remembering that {First Name} on {Street} said "call me after Labor Day" and actually doing it eleven weeks later — a record-keeping problem, not a sales problem. Log the outcome, the asking price, the showing count, and the exact callback date before you dial the next number; the note you don't write in the ten seconds after hanging up is the note that never exists. It also helps to call when people answer — see the best time to cold call, and remember most FSBO owners work a day job, so evenings and weekend mornings beat mid-morning.

Before you dial

Scrub your list against the national Do Not Call registry and your own internal do-not-call list, honor removal requests immediately and permanently, and stay inside the calling hours your state allows — several states are stricter than the federal window, and the hours follow the number's time zone, not yours. Some states and local boards add rules around calling for-sale-by-owner numbers and using automated dialing. Check the current rules where you call, and talk to your broker or an attorney about your own obligations.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best FSBO script for a first call?

The buyer-first opener works best on a first call, because it gives the owner something instead of asking for something. You mention that you work with buyers in the area, ask whether they would cooperate with an agent who brings one, and let the conversation open from there. It sidesteps the listing pitch that FSBOs are braced for.

How long should I keep calling a FSBO?

Plan on eight to twelve weeks. Most for-sale-by-owner sellers who do not sell solo eventually list with an agent, and the agent they call is usually the one who stayed in touch without pressure. A practical cadence is day one, day four, day ten, then every seven to ten days until the home sells or they list.

How do I answer the commission objection from a FSBO?

Agree with them first, then reframe the number as net proceeds rather than a fee. Tell them you are not asking them to pay anything today, and ask what they would do if a buyer came with an offer that netted them more after commission than their current asking price nets them now. That turns a price argument into a math question.

Should I call FSBOs and expired listings the same way?

No. An expired seller already hired an agent and was disappointed, so the call starts with acknowledgment and a different plan. A FSBO chose to avoid agents in the first place, so the call has to earn the right to be useful before it earns the right to pitch. The openers are not interchangeable.

When is the best time to call FSBO sellers?

FSBO owners are easiest to reach outside working hours, which is why late afternoon and early evening on weekdays tend to beat mid-morning, and why weekend mornings around open house time work well. Always call in the property owner time zone and stay inside the calling hours your state allows.

What should I say when a FSBO says "call me in the spring"?

Treat it as a scheduled follow-up, not a rejection. Ask what price range they are thinking, commit to one check-in before spring with anything that affects their number, and log the exact month. When you call back, lead with a comparable sale on their street rather than asking whether they have changed their mind.
